"Only broke people buy Gucci" is a common refrain you'll hear when people talking about the price of luxury fashion goods. It's a refrain that you hear from both middle class people shocked at the exaggerated swagger of someone with a mysterious amount of designer clothes, and you'll likely here it as well from wealthy people, especially those whose connection to their money comes from work. I've been wondering if this is true at all.

At face value, the claim sounds like status snobbery dressed up as financial wisdom. The suggestion is that "real" wealth is quiet, disciplined, and invisible, while conspicuous luxury spending is evidence of insecurity, debt, or misaligned priorities. Yet that framing collapses under even mild scrutiny. The reality is more complicated—and more uncomfortable—because it reveals a sharp, K-shaped divergence in how different income and wealth cohorts interact with luxury goods.

## Understanding the K-Shape

In a K-shaped economy, the top and bottom segments of the market diverge after a shock, while the middle stagnates or erodes. Applied to luxury, this produces a counterintuitive outcome: both the truly wealthy and the financially precarious participate in luxury consumption, but for fundamentally different reasons, with different constraints, and very different risk profiles.

At the bottom of the "luxury consumer" spectrum sit aspirational buyers. These are individuals for whom a $1,200 bag or $600 sneakers represent a significant percentage of disposable income—or are financed outright. For this group, luxury goods function less as durable assets and more as symbolic capital. The purchase is not about craftsmanship, longevity, or resale value; it is about visibility, identity signaling, and social legibility.

## The Collapsing Middle

Between these two groups is the collapsing middle: professionals with strong incomes but limited wealth accumulation, rising costs, and diminishing long-term security. This group is the most sensitive to luxury pricing—and the most vocal in condemning it. For them, luxury spending represents opportunity cost. A $3,000 bag is a mortgage payment, an index fund contribution, or a childcare expense.

> Their critique is not moral so much as defensive. Luxury feels irresponsible because, in their financial reality, it often is.

## What Brands Understand

Luxury brands understand this divergence better than their critics do. Over the past decade, pricing strategies have intentionally pushed entry-level luxury upward, extracting more from aspirational buyers while using scarcity, private access, and ultra-high-end lines to deepen relationships with the wealthy.

The uncomfortable truth is that luxury consumption is not a reliable proxy for wealth. It is a proxy for _how_ someone experiences the economy. In a K-shaped world, the same handbag can signify financial strain or financial indifference. The logo does not tell you which side of the curve someone is on.
